Ambarella (AMBA): The Memory Crunch Exposed a Business That Has Never Earned Its Stock Comp

Published 27 November 2026 — the full thesis, unchanged.

Why This Takes A Full Read

Ambarella closed Friday at $35.24, down 40.2% on the week and 62% below the $93.57 high of the past year. On Tuesday, 24 November, it reported third-quarter revenue of $115.2 million, up 6.2% and at the bottom of its guidance range, and guided the fourth quarter to $70 to $75 million, against roughly $112 million the Street expected. That is a 37% sequential drop in a quarter that is normally only seasonally soft. The cause, in CEO Fermi Wang's words, is "the unprecedented rise in memory prices, as well as the scarcity of memory supply, which is causing our customers, to different degrees, to cancel, push out, or delay new orders." The stock had closed at $55.56 on Monday. By Wednesday's close it was $35.68, after trading in the session at levels last seen in late 2018, and it finished the holiday-shortened week at $35.24.

That puts AMBA 46% below its 200-week moving average of about $65, deep in the territory this site calls extreme value. It crossed under the line in mid-October and has fallen further every week since. The record here is mixed and mostly bad. The stock has been under its line sixteen times, with an average return after touching of 32.5%, but the two long visits are the ones that resemble today: from January 2018 it spent 84 weeks under the line, fell 42% below it and was down 29% a year later; from February 2023 it spent 130 weeks under, fell 53% below and was down 31% a year later. The short, sharp visit of March 2020, which more than doubled in a year, came with a demand shock that reversed in months. What I want to know about this visit is which kind of shock memory is, and what the business looks like underneath it.

I went through the fiscal 2026 10-K, the second-quarter 10-Q, four quarterly releases, the 2026 proxy, the third-quarter call, and the reporting on the memory market and on NXP's reported takeover talks. Three things the headlines skipped decide the call. First, the memory squeeze is a bill-of-materials problem that hits Ambarella's customers harder than Ambarella's own chips, and management itself assumes DRAM prices stay roughly where they are through fiscal 2028. Second, Ambarella has reported a GAAP loss in every one of the last four fiscal years, because stock compensation of roughly $100 million a year, a quarter of revenue, is larger than the free cash flow it has ever produced. Third, the stock is still priced as if a buyer were coming: the Financial Times reported in July that NXP was in talks to acquire the company, no deal has followed, and the call this week said only that the board remains open if approached.

My call is that AMBA is a value trap at $35.24. My DCF, treating stock compensation as the cost it is, comes out at $22.91; the same model with stock compensation added back gives $39.31, which is roughly the price. The risk posture: a takeover offer, or a fast memory reversal, could send the stock up 50% overnight, and this call accepts that risk explicitly.

The Company: Twenty-Two Years of Replacing the Customer

Fermi Wang and Les Kohn founded Ambarella in early 2004, incorporated in the Cayman Islands and run from Santa Clara. Both came out of C-Cube Microsystems, the digital video chip company of the 1990s, and then co-founded Afara Websystems, which Sun Microsystems bought in 2002. Ambarella's first target was high-definition H.264 video compression. Its core competence for two decades has been the same thing: squeezing high-quality video processing into a chip that uses very little power.

The first reinvention was consumer video. Ambarella's chips went into camcorders, then into action cameras, and the action-camera boom made the company. It went public in October 2012 at $6 a share, 40% below the midpoint of its range. GoPro became its largest end customer, about 30% of revenue ($95 million of $316 million) in fiscal 2016, and the stock rose above $100 in 2015. Then GoPro moved to a replacement chip of its own starting in the second half of 2017, and Ambarella guided its share of fiscal 2018 revenue down to 3% to 6%. Losing the customer that built you is the founding trauma of this company, and it shaped everything that followed.

The second reinvention was computer vision. Ambarella paid $30 million for VisLab, an Italian autonomous-driving research group, in 2015, showed its first computer-vision chip, CV1, in 2017 and launched the CV2 family in 2018. Security cameras replaced action cameras as the core market. The older chips made video for people to watch; the new ones put an AI accelerator on the same die as the video engine, which is what lets a camera count people or read a license plate without sending video to the cloud.

The third reinvention is automotive and radar. In November 2021 Ambarella paid $307.5 million in cash for Oculii, a radar perception software company, and in January 2022 it announced the CV3 family of automotive domain controllers for driver assistance and autonomy. The stock closed at an all-time high of $216.84 on 8 December 2021, a week after a quarter that sent it up 25%. Five years later it trades at about a sixth of that.

Between the second and third reinventions sits the period that shaped today's balance sheet. Security cameras became Ambarella's largest market as action cameras faded, and the AI-capable CV2 family, announced in 2018 with up to 20 times the neural-network performance of CV1, was the product that carried the company through the 2018 to 2019 downturn, when the stock spent 84 weeks below its 200-week line. The CV5, a 5-nanometer chip that could encode 8K video at under two watts, followed in 2021. Each generation raised the average selling price, which is the lever the company has pulled every time unit growth stalled: the newest families sell for more than $20 against a company average of about $15. Ambarella wins on capability per watt and has always priced above the low-cost Asian suppliers. It gets paid for that only in products whose makers can afford it, which is the detail the memory shock is now testing.

The fourth reinvention, the current one, is "edge AI": N1 chips that run large language models on a device, CV7 on 4-nanometer silicon, CV8 on 2-nanometer, and a software platform for robotics and edge infrastructure. The record of revenue and profit shows what each reinvention cost:

The Management Team

Fermi Wang has been chairman, president and CEO since he co-founded the company in January 2004. Before that he was CEO and co-founder of Afara Websystems and spent nine years at C-Cube, finishing as a vice president and general manager. Twenty-two years of founder leadership is the main reason Ambarella survived losing GoPro, and it is also why the company has stayed small: the same team has run four product transitions without ever building a business that earns its own compensation. His pay in fiscal 2026 was $7.35 million, $5.93 million of it in stock, and his annual bonus is paid in fully vested shares.

Les Kohn, the co-founder and long-time CTO, stepped down as an executive officer and director in November 2025 and stays on as Chief Technology Advisor. Chan Lee (COO since 2021) and John Ju (SVP Systems) have both been with the company since its founding team in 2004, by way of Afara, Intel and C-Cube. The engineering leadership is unusually stable: the 10-K reports an average employee tenure of 8.1 years and 31% of staff with more than ten years at the company.

John Young became CFO in February 2024 after seven years in Ambarella's finance organization and seven at Mellanox. His credibility is now tied to the guidance record: the company guided Q1, Q2 and Q3 of this fiscal year to ranges of $97–103 million, $105–111 million and $115–124 million, and delivered $100.4 million, $108.1 million and $115.2 million. The first two landed in the middle. The third landed at the bottom, and the fourth will land 37% lower.

The board structure is founder-friendly: Wang is chairman as well as CEO, with a lead independent director. Insiders own about 4% of the shares. The compensation program pays executives mostly in restricted stock units and performance units tied to relative shareholder return and revenue growth, which means the people who decide whether to sell the company are paid in its stock.

The Memory Squeeze Is A Bill-Of-Materials Problem

The mechanism is worth getting precisely, because it decides whether this quarter is a one-time air pocket. Ambarella's chips sell at an average price of about $15, with the newest families above $20. The camera or drone that contains one also contains DRAM, often LPDDR. Through 2026, memory makers moved capacity to the high-bandwidth memory and server DRAM that AI data centers buy, and conventional DRAM contract prices rose by roughly 93–98% in the first quarter alone, according to TrendForce. TrendForce describes supplier inventories as extremely low and expects suppliers to add bits mainly through process migrations, because new cleanroom capacity takes years to build.

For a consumer camera maker, that means the memory in the product can now cost more than the processor, the retail price is fixed, and the choice is between shipping at a loss and not shipping. For Ambarella, it means orders for a chip whose own price has not changed get cancelled or pushed out because a different component doubled. Management said most of the reduction is push-outs, with some cancellations, and that it had "heavily scrutinized our customers' orders and the forecast, and removed items we do not expect to ship."

Three things in management's own framing make me cautious about a quick snapback. First, the company assumes DRAM stays roughly where it is through fiscal 2028. In Wang's words, "we are assuming that DRAM situation is going to stay the same as Q4." Second, it said the fiscal 2028 growth it expects comes mainly from new products ramping in the second half, and that recovered push-outs are "not big part of it." Third, the weakness started in consumer and is spreading to enterprise and automotive, which makes it a cost problem across the whole customer base. Against that, the memory equities have started to price a peak: Micron's stock fell sharply from late October into November on fears that pricing had topped, and TrendForce's November forecast had PC DRAM contract prices still rising in the fourth quarter, at a slower 13% to 18%. If memory prices break, Ambarella's push-outs come back fast. I give that a real probability, which is why my bull case exists.

One more effect, which the market has not yet discounted: in a market where every component is scarce, customers redesign around what they can get. A memory shortage that runs into 2028 rewards suppliers whose chips need less memory, and it rewards integrated vendors who can bundle memory supply. Ambarella is neither, and its largest competitors in mass-market cameras, Novatek and the Chinese suppliers, compete on cost.

The arithmetic of the promised "growth year" is the part I would most like readers to check for themselves. Fiscal 2027 revenue will be about $396 million if Q4 lands at the guide midpoint. If the first half of fiscal 2028 runs near the Q4 rate, call it $75 to $85 million a quarter, the first half totals $150 to $170 million. To grow for the year, the second half then has to deliver $226 to $246 million, or $113 to $123 million a quarter, which is at or above the record $115.2 million Ambarella just reported, achieved before the memory shock. That is possible if memory eases and the new chips ramp on time. It is a demanding bar for a company that has just told investors it expects memory to stay where it is, and it is why I model fiscal 2028 slightly down.

An illustration of the squeeze, with round numbers I chose to show the mechanism (none of them reported): take a consumer camera that costs its maker $60 to build and sells for $100, with $9 of memory and a $15 Ambarella chip inside. If the memory doubles to $18, the maker's cost rises 15% and its gross margin falls from 40% to 31%. A maker with thin margins either raises the retail price into a weak consumer market, accepts the lower margin, or stops building the product. In each case the Ambarella chip is the component least responsible for the problem and the one that stops shipping. Enterprise and automotive products carry more margin and absorb the same shock for longer, which matches the order in which management says the weakness has spread.

The cash position buys time. With $282.8 million of cash and marketable securities and no debt, Ambarella can absorb a year of the losses in my base case, about $52 million of negative free cash flow in fiscal 2028, without raising money. Even my bear case, which burns about $190 million over three years, leaves cash on hand. What a year of waiting does cost is about 6% of the company in stock compensation at today's price, and the recovery, when it comes, accrues partly to new shares.

Stock Compensation Is The Business Model

Ambarella reports a non-GAAP profit most quarters and a GAAP loss every year. The gap between the two is almost entirely stock compensation: $98.0 million in fiscal 2026 on $390.7 million of revenue, 25% of sales, and $111 million a year in the two years before that. In Q3 FY2027, non-GAAP net income was $12.7 million and GAAP net loss was $12.7 million; the $25.4 million difference is stock compensation and acquisition costs.

The comparison that matters is stock compensation against free cash flow, because that is the cost of the engineers set against the cash the business produces:

Fiscal 2026 was the best year in the table: revenue up 37%, edge AI demand strong, gross margin near 60%. It still did not cover its stock compensation. That is the structural fact the memory shock exposed. A 959-person company, 75% of it engineers, designing two or three new chips a year on leading-edge Samsung nodes, has a fixed cost base of about $234 million a year in cash operating expenses plus roughly $90 million in stock. At 60% gross margin it needs about $540 million of annual revenue to break even on a GAAP basis. It has never been there; its record year was fiscal 2026, at $391 million.

The dilution is real and measured: about 3.5% a year of share count growth with almost no offsetting buybacks. An investor who values Ambarella on non-GAAP earnings is paying for engineers with someone else's shares. My DCF charges stock compensation as a cost, and that one choice is worth about $16 a share: $22.91 with it, $39.30 without.

The Buyer In The Price

In June 2025 Bloomberg reported that Ambarella was working with bankers and had approached potential buyers, and the stock rose about 20%. On 31 July 2026 the Financial Times reported that NXP Semiconductors was in talks to acquire the company, citing people familiar with the matter, with no terms disclosed; Ambarella was worth about $3.25 billion before the report, and the stock rose about 16% that day. NXP declined to comment and Ambarella did not respond. The FT said the talks might not lead to a transaction and that other industry players could also bid. Later reports of exclusive talks and named rival bidders I could not verify, and I give them no weight.

No deal has been announced in the four months since. On this week's call, when Susquehanna asked about strategic alternatives, Wang said the board's view had not changed: the company is open if approached and otherwise runs independently, and the memory issue does not change that. He did not mention NXP.

The strategic logic for a buyer like NXP is real. NXP sells automotive processors and radar, and Ambarella's vision SoCs and Oculii radar software would let it offer a fuller perception package. A buyer would also cut most of the duplicated overhead and stop paying the stock compensation in Ambarella's currency. But the timing works against a premium. Four months ago a buyer was looking at a company growing revenue 13% with Q3 guided to $115–124 million. Today it is looking at a Q4 guided 37% lower and a management team that does not forecast memory relief before 2028. A rational acquirer waits for the fourth-quarter print, or offers a price that reflects it.

I treat the takeover as an option with a 10% weight at $50 a share, about 5.2 times my fiscal 2027 revenue estimate in enterprise value. That is a premium to today's price and well below what the July valuation implied. Even generously, the option adds only about $2.70 to my anchor. The current price makes sense to me only if you believe a deal at a premium is much more likely than that, or if you value the company ignoring its stock compensation.

The comparison mostly shows that the market's generosity toward Ambarella's multiple depends on the story, and that a vision-chip company without growth gets valued like Mobileye.

The Risks

The bull case is mostly upside risk to my call. A takeover offer from NXP or anyone else would likely come at a premium to $35 even after this week, and the stock would jump. My 10% weight on a $50 deal may be too low. The memory market could also break sooner than management assumes, returning push-outs in two or three quarters.

Memory stays expensive into 2028. Management assumes DRAM stays roughly where it is through fiscal 2028, and TrendForce still had contract prices rising in the fourth quarter. In that world consumer IoT demand stays impaired, customers redesign around scarce memory, and the 37% drop in Q4 becomes the run rate for longer than my base case allows.

Distributor and customer concentration. WT Microelectronics handled 56.7% of Q3 revenue and about 70% of fiscal 2026 revenue. Receivable days rose from 32 to 47 in Q3, and receivables jumped to $58.3 million from $37.4 million. Inventory of $61.4 million at the end of October is about six months of cost of sales at the Q4 guide. A change in that relationship, a write-down of chips built for orders that were pushed out, or a buildup of inventory in the channel that the company does not see, would hit revenue with little warning.

Stock compensation and dilution. Roughly $90–100 million a year of stock compensation against a market cap of $1.66 billion is about 6% of the company per year at today's price. If the share price stays here, the same dollar grants issue twice as many shares as they did in July.

Automotive takes longer still. CV3-AD was announced in 2022. If a major OEM platform win does not materialize by fiscal 2028, the automotive part of the story reverts to a telematics camera business, and the radar acquisition's $303.6 million of goodwill becomes an impairment candidate.

Cost competition in cameras. Novatek, SigmaStar and other Asian suppliers have taken share in mass-market security cameras on price. A memory shortage that squeezes customer margins pushes them toward cheaper processors, which is the opposite of Ambarella's strategy of selling higher-priced AI chips.

Leading-edge execution. CV7 on 4nm and CV8 on 2nm at Samsung are expensive tape-outs for a $400 million company. A delay or yield problem in the products management is counting on for the second half of fiscal 2028 would push the recovery out a year.

The Path From Here

Ambarella makes good chips. Its video and vision processors are in tens of millions of cameras, its gross margin has held near 60% through every transition, it has $283 million of cash and no borrowings, and its engineering team has stayed together for two decades. The silicon is as good as it was a week ago.

This week made the economics visible. A company with a record year of $391 million of revenue that still could not cover $98 million of stock compensation is now guiding a quarter 37% lower and expects no help from memory prices until 2028. My base case, which gives it a full recovery and doubled revenue by fiscal 2033, is worth $22.91 a share. The price of $35.24 makes sense only with stock compensation added back or with a buyer on the way.

So I call it a value trap at this price. It looks cheap: 46% below its 200-week line, 62% off its high, at levels last traded in late 2018. It is still above what its cash economics support. Bear case

The steelman, given equal room. My call already leans bear, so this tab argues the short case harder than I do.

The short thesis

Ambarella is a 22-year-old chip designer that has reinvented itself four times and has not once produced a business that pays for itself. Every fiscal year in the last four ended in a GAAP loss, including the record year, when revenue rose 37% and still fell $40 million short of covering stock compensation. The engineering team is excellent and expensive, and the company pays it by issuing about 3.5% more shares every year. At $35 that dilution costs public holders about 6% a year before the business earns anything.

Now the demand has broken in the way that hurts most. Memory makers have moved capacity to AI data centers, DRAM prices roughly doubled in a quarter, and Ambarella's customers, mostly makers of consumer and enterprise cameras with fixed retail prices, are cancelling or delaying products because the memory beside the chip has doubled in price. Management guided the next quarter 37% lower, says the weakness is spreading from consumer to enterprise and automotive, and assumes memory stays expensive through fiscal 2028. Its promise of a "growth year" rests on new products ramping in the second half, with recovered push-outs "not big part of it," so the growth is measured against a collapsed base. A first half running near $70 million a quarter puts fiscal 2028 revenue below fiscal 2026 even with a strong second half.

The automotive story, which justified paying $307.5 million for a radar software company, is still waiting: nearly five years after the CV3 announcement there is no volume OEM platform, and the Continental program is tied to 2027 launches that now have to source memory too. Mobileye, the incumbent, trades at 1.2 times revenue. In cameras, Chinese and Taiwanese suppliers compete on price, and a memory squeeze pushes customers toward the cheapest processor that works. More than half of revenue runs through one distributor, and receivable days rose by half in the quarter demand fell.

The cash story is weaker than the balance sheet looks. In the first half of fiscal 2027, with revenue growing 15%, free cash flow was −$36.7 million because inventory more than doubled from a year earlier. Inventory is still $61.4 million, about six months of cost of sales at the Q4 guide, and chips built for pushed-out orders are the first candidates for a write-down. At the guided cost base the company loses about $38 million a quarter on a GAAP basis and has announced no cuts. My base case quietly assumes about $40 million a year of savings; take them out of every year and the same model is worth about $15 a share, and the bear case burns about $190 million of the $283 million of cash by fiscal 2030.

That leaves the takeover, which is what the stock is really trading on. The FT's July report came when Ambarella was worth $3.3 billion with growing revenue. A buyer looking now sees a company guiding to a $290 million annual run rate, with management expecting no memory relief for more than a year. The rational move is to wait for the March print. If no bid comes, the stock is valued on its fundamentals, and on GAAP fundamentals, my bear case is worth about $7.74, mostly cash.

Why the re-rate may never fire

The stock has spent long stretches below its 200-week line twice before, 84 weeks from 2018 and 130 weeks from 2023, and both times it was lower a year later. Each time the company pointed to the next product generation as the recovery. The market has learned to discount that promise, and memory gives it a new reason to wait. A recovery needs memory prices to fall, customers to restart designs, and the new chips to ramp on schedule, all within about a year, while stock compensation keeps diluting every quarter it takes. And if a buyer offers a modest premium to a depressed price, the board may accept it, which would cap the upside for anyone buying today in hope of the July valuation.
