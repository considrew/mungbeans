NVIDIA (NVDA): Seven Times Earnings, Priced for a Normal Chip Cycle

Published 20 November 2026 — the full thesis, unchanged.

Why This Takes A Full Read

NVIDIA closed Friday at $125.53, down 22.9% on the week and 47.5% below its 6 October closing high of $239.24. It was the stock's worst week since October 2008. Three things happened in eight days. On 12 November the board announced that Jensen Huang, co-founder and chief executive since 1993, had resigned, four days after he admitted in a memo to employees that he had been unfaithful to his wife, amid press reports tying the relationship to a Singapore customer that US authorities had been investigating over chip diversion to China. On 17 November the company reported a third quarter with revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103%, and said it would file its 10-Q "in the coming weeks" instead of the same day, as it usually does. The same day the Wall Street Journal reported an SEC investigation, and on Friday Reuters reported that the SEC's enforcement staff were examining NVIDIA's accounting. The stock fell 14% on Friday alone, its seventh straight weekly loss.

That leaves NVDA 1.8% above its 200-week moving average of $123.33, the line this site is built around. It has not closed below it since September 2022, when it spent five weeks underneath, bottomed 12.5% below, and was up 233% a year later. The two long visits before that were the bad ones: from June 2002 the stock spent 139 weeks under the line and fell as far as 69% below it, and from June 2008 it spent 131 weeks under and fell 65% below. Twelve visits in all, an average return of 54% after touching, and a spread between outcomes wide enough to make the average useless on its own. What decides which kind of visit this is comes down to two questions: whether the earnings are real, and whether they are a peak.

I went through the fiscal 2026 10-K, the second-quarter 10-Q, the third-quarter release, the CFO commentary and the supplemental financial information NVIDIA posted in place of the 10-Q, the August 8-K on the Ohio data center guarantee, and the reporting on Huang's exit and the SEC probe. Here is what I found. On the first question, the reported numbers hold together better than the headlines suggest, but the balance sheet behind them has changed shape: in nine months NVIDIA spent $58.1 billion buying equity in its own ecosystem, against $113.7 billion of free cash flow, and it now guarantees up to $118 billion of other parties' obligations. On the second question, 7.6 times next year's consensus earnings is a peak-cycle multiple, and my DCF says the price already assumes a normal semiconductor downturn somewhere in the next four years.

My call is wait. At $125.53 the stock trades almost exactly at my probability-weighted value. The 10-Q, due around 4 December, and Huang's transcribed interview with House committees, reported for 1 December, are dated events that will tell me more than any model can today. The risk posture: this is the world's most widely held growth stock, it fell 14% in a day this week, and it can rise just as fast.

The Company: Thirty-Three Years of Betting the Company

NVIDIA was incorporated in California in April 1993 by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. Huang had designed microprocessors at AMD and spent eight years at LSI Logic. The company's history is a sequence of bets large enough to sink it, and the capital allocation story is how each one paid for the next.

The first bet was that PC graphics would become its own chip category. The early products struggled; the RIVA line in the late 1990s worked, and in 1999 NVIDIA went public and launched the GeForce 256, which it marketed as the first GPU. Gaming funded the company for the next fifteen years, and the gaming franchise is still there: $16.0 billion of revenue in fiscal 2026.

The second bet, and the one that made the company, was CUDA in 2006: a programming model that let developers use the GPU for general-purpose parallel computing. For years it cost money and earned little. NVIDIA put CUDA on every consumer GPU it sold, which meant every student and researcher with a gaming card had a parallel computer and the software to program it. In 2012 the AlexNet network, trained on NVIDIA GPUs, won the ImageNet competition. The company calls that moment the "Big Bang" of AI, and it is the origin of everything since. By the end of fiscal 2026 NVIDIA said it had spent more than $76.7 billion on research and development since inception, and more than half its engineers work on software.

The third bet was buying the network. NVIDIA closed the roughly $7 billion acquisition of Mellanox in 2020, which gave it InfiniBand and the DPU, and turned it from a chip vendor into a company that sells a whole data center. Networking revenue reached $31.4 billion in fiscal 2026. The attempt to go further, a $40 billion purchase of Arm announced in 2020, died under regulatory opposition in early 2022. The stock fell from $33.23 in November 2021 to $11.19 in October 2022 (split-adjusted) as crypto demand collapsed and the post-pandemic PC boom ended. That decline is the most recent reminder that this is a cyclical business. Seven weeks after the low, ChatGPT launched.

The fourth bet is the one being graded now: the annual cadence. NVIDIA moved to a new data center architecture every year (Hopper, Blackwell, Blackwell Ultra, and now Vera Rubin, which began shipping this quarter in what the company calls its fastest ramp ever), and it began selling whole racks instead of chips. Revenue went from $27.0 billion in fiscal 2023 to $60.9 billion, $130.5 billion and $215.9 billion in fiscal 2026. The nine months to October 2026 brought in $293.7 billion.

A company that for thirty years kept a fortress balance sheet and let customers finance their own purchases has, in eighteen months, taken on $25 billion of new debt, committed up to $105 billion of credit support for a customer's data center, and put tens of billions into the equity of the companies that buy its chips. Each move has a defensible logic. Together they mean the next downturn will hit a balance sheet built for the upturn.

The Management Team

Jensen Huang is gone. He resigned as president, CEO and director on 12 November, effective immediately. On 8 November he apologized to employees, writing "I was unfaithful to my wife" and "There is no excuse for what I did." The New York Times reported on 29 October that US officials believe the woman involved, a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore company that bought billions of dollars of NVIDIA chips, was a Chinese intelligence asset. China called the claim "purely unsubstantiated," Attorney General Todd Blanche said on 12 November that no conclusion had been reached on whether she was a spy, Singapore said it lacked evidence to charge her and barred her from re-entering the country, and NVIDIA said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. On 11 November the Times also reported a relationship with an NVIDIA executive who reported to him. The board's independent investigation is run by former acting US attorney Joon Kim and Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, and on the earnings call the new chair said the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." I will not try to adjudicate any of it. What matters for the stock is that the founder who set the architecture cadence, ran the customer relationships personally and was the company's public voice for 33 years is out, and that his exit is entangled with export-control questions Congress now wants answered.

Colette Kress is interim CEO and remains CFO. She joined in September 2013 after senior finance roles at Cisco, Microsoft and Texas Instruments, and she has run NVIDIA's finance organization through the entire AI ramp. She opened and closed this week's call with the same line: "Our momentum is real." She is the obvious steward and an unusual choice to keep both jobs during a quarter in which the 10-Q is late and the SEC is reportedly looking at the accounting she signs.

Harvey Jones, a former CEO and executive chairman of Synopsys and a long-time director, became NVIDIA's first-ever board chair. Until this month NVIDIA had no board chair at all. The Wall Street Journal reported that Heidrick & Struggles is running the CEO search, with internal and external candidates. On 17 November the board formed a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, chaired by director Suzanne Nora Johnson, to which the company's national security and trade compliance teams now report.

Below them sits a bench that has been in place for a decade or more. Jay Puri (71) runs worldwide field operations, which means the hyperscaler relationships. Debora Shoquist (71) runs operations and the supply chain that has to absorb $390 billion of purchase commitments. Tim Teter (59) is general counsel and is reportedly due to sit for his own transcribed interview with House committees on 2 December. The ages matter. The three executives who run sales, supply and legal are between 59 and 71, and the CEO search will decide whether NVIDIA's next decade is run by its current generation or a new one.

Two data points on alignment. Director Mark Stevens sold about 4.2 million shares for roughly $946 million at $220 to $234 in early and mid-September, near the top. And President Trump said on Friday, of Huang, "I would love to see Jensen be given a second chance," and appeared to criticize the people now running it. A sitting president lobbying publicly over a CEO succession is a governance variable I have never had to model before.

Half The Free Cash Flow Goes Back Out The Door

The bull case says NVIDIA earns $65 billion a quarter and buys back stock with it. The cash flow statement says something more complicated. Over the nine months to 25 October, NVIDIA generated $122.7 billion of operating cash flow and $113.7 billion of free cash flow. It used that cash, and the $24.9 billion it borrowed in June, like this:

The equity purchases are the line to watch. NVIDIA's non-marketable equity holdings, stakes in private companies carried at cost plus observable price changes, went from $22.3 billion in January to $65.5 billion in October, with $46.4 billion of net additions in nine months. The CFO commentary says the equity investments are "focused on frontier labs, infrastructure financiers, and other private companies." Frontier labs and infrastructure financiers are the customer base. Its publicly held equity securities rose to $48.9 billion, $21.0 billion of it under lock-up. Equity gains contributed $25.5 billion to the nine months' $220.4 billion of pre-tax income.

Then add what sits off the balance sheet. The CFO commentary lists $518 billion of commitments: $390 billion of supply and capacity (up from $279 billion a quarter earlier, mostly multi-year memory), $28 billion of cloud service agreements, $74 billion of data center leases not yet commenced with terms up to twenty years, $20 billion of committed equity investments and $6 billion of capital spending. On top of that are $32 billion of contingent neocloud agreements and $118 billion of maximum guarantee exposure, $105 billion of it the credit support for SB Energy's Ohio campus, which SB Energy will build and lease to an OpenAI affiliate for 20 years.

All of this is disclosed, in more detail than most companies give, and each arrangement has a business reason: secure memory before rivals do, help customers who cannot yet borrow at scale, lock a 4.25-gigawatt campus to NVIDIA hardware. Kress told analysts this week that the company would be far more selective about financing customers. The point for valuation is that these arrangements change what the earnings mean. A dollar of revenue from a hyperscaler paying in 60 days is worth more than a dollar of revenue from a neocloud that NVIDIA has invested in, rents capacity back from, and guarantees the data center for. In the second case part of the revenue's risk stays on NVIDIA's balance sheet, and that risk shows up in a downturn.

The receivables line points the same way. Accounts receivable were $81.0 billion at 25 October, 64 days of sales, up from 60 days a quarter earlier and from $38.5 billion in January. The company attributes the rise to Rubin shipment timing and "extended payment terms on large, multi-quarter agreements with certain investment-grade customers." Receivables absorbed $42.6 billion of cash in nine months, and inventory another $18.4 billion, with raw materials up from $3.8 billion to $15.8 billion. Operating cash flow was 67% of net income for the nine months. Strip out the equity gains and it is closer to 75%. That is a lower conversion rate than a company with these margins usually shows, and it is the gap a skeptic points to.

My read: the cash is real, the customers are mostly real, and the conversion gap is mostly a growth artifact. What has changed is that NVIDIA now carries part of the AI buildout's credit risk itself. I value that by charging an "ecosystem investment" cost of 5% of revenue against free cash flow in my model through fiscal 2029, and 3% after, and by leaving the $65.5 billion of private stakes out of my cash.

What The SEC Can And Cannot Change

Two things about the probe are known. The Wall Street Journal reported on 17 November that the SEC was investigating whether NVIDIA's disclosures misled investors, and Reuters reported on 20 November, citing two people briefed on the matter, that enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues. Reuters said it could not determine what those issues are or when the inquiry began. The SEC declined to comment and NVIDIA did not respond. Many SEC investigations close without any action.

The candidates the press has raised fall into two groups, and the distinction matters for the stock. The first is presentation. In the third quarter of fiscal 2026 NVIDIA switched its geographic revenue disclosure from billing location to customer headquarters, which removed Singapore, then a large billing hub, from the breakdown. The Financial Times reported that Singapore sales had grown to almost half of revenue before the change. NVIDIA said the change would better reflect the geographic profile of its revenue, and in 2025 said that over 99% of data center compute revenue billed to Singapore was for US-based customers. A finding here, at worst, means restated geographic disclosures and possibly a penalty. It does not change total revenue, margins or cash. Its real risk is political: it feeds the export-control story that already cost a CEO.

The second group is recognition: when and how much revenue gets booked. Extended payment terms, rising receivables, multi-quarter agreements, customers that NVIDIA also invests in and rents from. Any of these could raise questions about revenue timing or about whether some sales and investments should be accounted for together. There is no reporting that the SEC is looking at any of them. But this is the group that could change the numbers, and the late 10-Q is why the market cannot rule it out. NVIDIA told UBS analyst Tim Arcuri that the delay relates to whether additional risk factors about the Huang investigation need to be added to the filing. That is a plausible and benign reason. The filing itself will be the evidence: if the 10-Q arrives by its deadline, around 4 December, with the same numbers as the supplemental document and no change to revenue recognition policy or auditor language, the recognition question mostly closes. If it arrives late, or with changes, it opens.

I give the second outcome a low probability, because the cash collected supports the revenue: $122.7 billion of operating cash flow in nine months is hard to fake, and a 64-day DSO is within normal range for a hardware company. I do not give it zero, and my bust scenario below is sized for it.

Seven Times Earnings Is A Cycle Question

The headline multiple is the bull's best argument. At $125.53, NVDA trades at 7.6 times the consensus forward earnings of $16.61 a share and about 12 times my estimate of fiscal 2027 earnings of $10.40. No company this profitable trades this cheap unless the market thinks the earnings are about to fall.

NVIDIA's history says that after every great boom the earnings fell hard. In fiscal 2023 revenue was flat and net income fell 55% as crypto and PC demand broke. In late 2018 the stock fell 56% in three months on a crypto inventory glut. In 2008 it spent 131 weeks under its 200-week line. A data center business sold to a handful of buyers whose capital budgets move together is more concentrated than any of those, and today's operating margin of 67% is a record. The Q4 guide already brings gross margin down to 71–72% from 74.8% as Rubin ramps.

The other side of the ledger: Q4 revenue guidance of $130 billion in a quarter with an extra week, $390 billion of supply commitments that only make sense against much larger revenue, customers who, by accounts of the call, are still asking to increase shipments, and a product cadence that has so far kept each architecture's pricing intact. Wedbush's Dan Ives, who cut his target to $225 from $300, summed up the bull view: "Demand did not leave with him."

My base case splits the difference: fiscal 2028 revenue up 50% (consensus implies something closer to 70%), then a slowdown and a 15% decline in fiscal 2030 as the buildout digests, with operating margins drifting to 50%. That is a normal semiconductor cycle at an abnormal scale. On those assumptions the stock is worth about what it trades for. That is the finding of this piece. The market is pricing a normal cycle, and the debate from here is about whether the cycle will be normal.

Two variants frame it. A consensus-like path (fiscal 2028 revenue up 70%, no down year, margins easing slowly to 55%) gives $209.53 at the same discount rate. A bust (revenue up 5% next year, then two years down 25% each, margins at 40%, as an accounting restatement and a capex freeze would combine to produce) gives $43.40. I weight them 25% bust, 50% base, 25% consensus, which gives about $128, within 2% of the price.

Multiple sanity check. At $125.53 the equity is worth $3.05 trillion and the enterprise value, net of my $70 billion, about $2.98 trillion. That is 19 times my estimate of fiscal 2027 free cash flow of about $159 billion, 12 times fiscal 2027 earnings of about $10.40 a share (about $9.50 excluding equity gains), and 7.6 times consensus fiscal 2028 earnings. AMD, the closest listed peer, trades at about 24 times forward earnings. On any trailing measure NVDA is cheap. On a forward measure it is cheap only if consensus is right about fiscal 2028.

Three things stand out. First, scale: NVIDIA's data center business is about 13 times AMD's, and NVIDIA's quarterly operating income is about six times AMD's quarterly revenue. Second, the margin gap of 20 points of gross margin is the clearest measure of NVIDIA's software and system moat; AMD wins business by being the second source, which hyperscalers want, at a lower price. Third, the market now values AMD at 24 times forward earnings and NVIDIA at 7.6 times, the inverse of their competitive positions. That gap is the market saying it believes AMD's growth is earlier in its cycle and NVIDIA's is near its peak, plus a governance discount on NVIDIA that did not exist a month ago.

What would AMD need to close the gap? A software ecosystem that makes moving off CUDA cheap, rack-scale reliability at hyperscale volume, and customers willing to standardize on it for training as well as inference. It is gaining on all three, and MI450's customer list is the strongest AMD has had. It is still years behind on the first. For NVIDIA's valuation, AMD's main effect is a cap on pricing: every Helios deployment gives a hyperscaler leverage in its next NVIDIA negotiation, and that leverage shows up first in gross margin.

The Risks

The SEC probe touches recognition. If the investigation turns out to concern when or how revenue is booked on multi-quarter agreements, extended terms or transactions with investees, a restatement of timing is possible and the multiple would fall before anyone knew the size. The 10-Q, due around 4 December, is the first evidence either way. A late filing would be a red flag in itself.

The cycle turns. Three direct customers accounted for 36% of Q3 revenue, the capital budgets behind them move together, and the operating margin is at a record. Every prior NVIDIA boom ended with earnings falling by half or more. My base case includes one down year; a second, 10% decline would take it to about $116.

Credit risk moved onto the balance sheet. $118 billion of maximum guarantee exposure, $65.5 billion of private equity stakes, $32 billion of neocloud commitments and $28 billion of cloud services NVIDIA rents back. In a downturn these lose value at the same moment revenue falls, and the guarantees can turn into cash payments.

Leadership. The founder who ran product cadence and the largest customer relationships is gone, the interim CEO is also the CFO, the senior bench is 59 to 71, and the permanent CEO search runs while Congress, the SEC and the President all weigh in. Customer and employee retention through that is not guaranteed. RSUs granted this year at an average grant-date value of $183.74 have lost about a third of their value, which tests retention.

China and export politics. The Q4 guide assumes zero data center compute revenue from China. On 16 November the Commerce Department issued a rule requiring a license for any data center, anywhere, to rent large amounts of US-chip compute to Chinese companies, and added license requirements for advanced chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil. The Megaspeed investigation, congressional hearings and the President's own comments all keep export policy volatile, in either direction, and a hostile turn could also touch Taiwan-headquartered customers that are 26% of revenue.

Gross margin compression. Q4 is guided to 71–72%, down three points, on the Rubin ramp. Memory costs are rising (supply commitments are up $111 billion in a quarter, mostly memory), and AMD's MI450 gives buyers a second source. Three points lower margins in every year of my model would take the value to about $121.

Customer concentration and in-house chips. The hyperscalers, 52% of revenue this quarter, design their own accelerators. Each generation they shift more inference work to them, so the depth of the next down cycle depends on NVIDIA's share of their spending as much as on the total.

The Path From Here

NVIDIA is still the most important company in the AI buildout. It earned $65.3 billion in the quarter it reported this week, it guided to $130 billion of revenue next quarter, and its products are the default choice at every major lab and cloud. That position survived the week intact.

The price and what it assumes moved instead. At $125.53 the market is no longer paying for the buildout to continue indefinitely. My base case, which includes a normal downturn and margins falling to 50%, comes out at $129. The probability-weighted value, including a bust that would come with a restatement, comes out at about $128. The stock is fairly priced for a normal semiconductor cycle, sitting on its 200-week line, with a governance and accounting overhang that has dates attached.

So I wait, and I say exactly for what. I would buy on either of two things. A clean 10-Q, filed on time, with numbers matching the supplemental document, no change to revenue recognition and no new language from the auditor, would remove the bust tail and put the weighted value near $150. Or a price under about $100, my value at a 12% discount rate, would pay me for the uncertainty without the filing.

Bear case

The steelman, given equal room.

The short thesis

NVIDIA's earnings are a peak, and part of the peak is financed by NVIDIA. The company that once let customers pay their own way now buys equity in them ($58.1 billion in nine months, $65.5 billion of private stakes on the books), rents capacity back from them ($28 billion of cloud service agreements), commits to fill their data centers ($32 billion of neocloud agreements), and guarantees the buildings ($118 billion of maximum exposure, $105 billion of it for one customer's campus). Receivables doubled in nine months to $81 billion on "extended payment terms." Operating cash flow covered only two-thirds of net income, and $25.5 billion of the pre-tax income came from marking up equity stakes in the ecosystem. The marks have already started to turn: the third quarter carried a $1.8 billion impairment on private stakes, in a quarter when the buildout was still accelerating. A bear only has to show that the quality of each dollar of revenue has fallen while the market still capitalizes it as if every dollar came from a hyperscaler paying on normal terms.

Then the governance collapse. The founder resigned amid reports linking his personal life to a customer under export-control investigation. The board that let him run the company for 33 years without a chair is now running a CEO search under the eyes of three House committees, the SEC, California regulators and a President who wants Huang back. The interim CEO is the CFO whose accounting the SEC is reportedly examining, and the company did something it had not done in this cycle: it reported earnings without filing the 10-Q. Late filings precede restatements often enough that the market is right to treat this one as a signal until proven otherwise.

Then the cycle. Every NVIDIA boom has ended with earnings falling by half or more: fiscal 2023, late 2018, 2008. This one is concentrated in four or five buyers whose capital budgets move together, each designing its own chips, with AMD's MI450 now running at OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Anthropic and Oracle. Gross margin is already guided down three points. Memory commitments rose $111 billion in a single quarter, which means NVIDIA has locked in costs at peak prices for years. When the buildout pauses, revenue falls, the inventory and purchase commitments become liabilities, the equity stakes get written down, and the guarantees start to bite. Washington is adding a demand risk of its own. On 16 November the Commerce Department answered the Megaspeed story with a rule that requires a license for any data center renting large blocks of US-chip compute to Chinese companies, and for advanced chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil. China already contributes nothing to the Q4 guide; the rule puts part of the Southeast Asian demand that grew up around it in doubt as well. On those inputs my bust case is worth $43, and the bear would argue I give it too little weight.

Why the re-rate may never fire

The bull case needs the market to pay a growth multiple again for a company it now suspects is at a cyclical peak. That rarely happens until the cycle has turned and recovered, which in 2008 and 2018 took one to two years. Meanwhile the news flow for the next three months is regulatory, legal and political: a late 10-Q, congressional testimony, an SEC inquiry with no timetable, a CEO search, and export policy set by a President who is publicly arguing with the board. Each of those can only add uncertainty. AMD at 24 times earnings shows the market still pays up for AI growth it trusts. It prices NVIDIA's at 7.6 times, and nothing on the calendar before February is built to change that.
